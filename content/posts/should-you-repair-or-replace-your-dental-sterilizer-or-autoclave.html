Sterilizers sit in a category by themselves in this series, because a failure here isn't just an operational inconvenience — it's a compliance and patient-safety issue the moment it happens. That changes how conservatively you should treat the repair-or-replace line compared to a compressor or a handpiece.

Why the Threshold Should Be Lower Here Than Other Equipment

For most equipment categories in this series, we've suggested repairing up to roughly 50–60% of replacement cost. For sterilizers, that threshold should come down — closer to 35–40% — for one reason: a sterilizer that's failing intermittently, even in ways that don't trip a failed biological indicator today, represents a risk category that other equipment doesn't. A compressor that's slowly failing loses you production. A sterilizer that's slowly failing risks an infection-control finding, a failed state inspection, or worse. The cost of being wrong is asymmetric, and the repair-or-replace math should reflect that.

Most tabletop autoclaves are built for a 7–10 year service life at typical practice sterilization volume. Larger cassette or chamber sterilizers used in higher-volume practices or surgical settings can run 10–15 years but also see more cycles per day, so age alone isn't the full picture — cycle count matters as much as calendar age, similar to mileage mattering more than model year for a vehicle.

The One Rule That Overrides All the Others

If a unit has produced a failed spore test (biological indicator) more than once in any rolling 90-day period, it should be replaced, not repaired — regardless of what the repair would cost or how old the unit is. A recurring failed BI test means you can't currently trust the unit's core function, and no repair estimate changes that risk calculus. This is the one category in this entire series where age and repair cost take a back seat to a hard compliance signal.

Documentation: The Part of This Decision Most Practices Skip

Whatever you decide — repair or replace — document it. If you repair a sterilizer, keep the service record showing what was repaired, when, and the BI test results before and after. If you replace it, keep the disposition record of the outgoing unit (date removed from service, reason, and either resale/donation/disposal documentation). This isn't just good practice — it's exactly the kind of documentation an infection-control audit or an insurance claim after a loss event will ask for, and it's far easier to produce at the time of the decision than to reconstruct two years later.

What This Means for CAPEX Planning

Sterilizers are a smaller line-item purchase than chairs or imaging equipment, which is exactly why they're easy to under-plan for at the DSO level — a $3,000–$8,000 unit doesn't trigger the same budget scrutiny a $40,000 CBCT replacement does, so sterilizer replacement often happens reactively, one location at a time, instead of on a planned rotation. At scale, that reactive pattern is where compliance risk quietly accumulates: locations running the oldest, least-reliable units are often the ones nobody flagged because no single failure was ever expensive enough to escalate.
