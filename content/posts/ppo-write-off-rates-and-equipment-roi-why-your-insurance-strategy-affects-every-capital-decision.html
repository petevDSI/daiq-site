The relationship between PPO write-off rates and equipment investment decisions is one of the least discussed but most consequential dynamics in dental practice finance.

Here's the core tension: PPO write-off rates determine effective revenue per procedure. Effective revenue per procedure determines what new equipment investment can be justified. A practice writing off 42% of its PPO production is operating with dramatically compressed margins relative to an FFS or low-write-off practice — and that compression affects every capital expenditure decision the practice makes.

The Write-Off Rate Benchmark — Where Practices Actually Stand

The DSI Benchmark Index tracks PPO write-off rate (D2) as a core metric. National distribution for GP practices:

| Percentile | PPO Write-Off Rate | |---|---| | P10 | 15% | | P25 | 22% | | P50 (median) | 30% | | P75 | 38% | | P90 | 46%+ |

The top quartile of practices on this metric — those below 22% write-off — have either very selective PPO participation, successfully renegotiated fees, or a higher FFS mix that dilutes their overall write-off rate.

How Write-Off Rate Affects Equipment ROI Calculations

Every equipment ROI calculation in dentistry involves revenue assumptions. Those assumptions are very different at a 20% write-off rate vs. a 40% write-off rate.

Example: Intraoral scanner ROI

A practice considering an intraoral scanner (approximately $35,000–$55,000 depending on system) typically models the return as:

  • Reduced per-case lab cost ($60–$120/case savings vs. PVS impression + model)
  • Eliminated remakes (1–3% of cases)
  • New procedure capability (digital denture workflows, implant scanning, etc.)

At a 25% PPO write-off rate, the lab savings accrue at close to full value because the practice is collecting ~75 cents on the dollar from PPO procedures. At a 42% write-off rate, the savings are the same but the practice's overall revenue per procedure is compressed — which means the payback period on the scanner is longer and the opportunity cost of the capital is higher.

The compressor replacement example

A compressor replacement ($8,000–$18,000 depending on system and location count) is a necessity, not an efficiency investment — but the timing decision involves the same capital allocation question. A practice with compressed margins from high write-off rates may defer necessary equipment replacement in ways that create downstream risk: higher maintenance costs, unexpected downtime, and patient experience issues that affect retention.

Payor Mix as a Capital Planning Variable

Payor mix — the split between FFS, PPO, and Medicaid (metrics D1a, D1b, D1c) — is typically treated as an insurance strategy question. It's also a capital planning variable.

Why it matters for equipment investment:

FFS patients generate more revenue per procedure than PPO patients at the same fee schedule, because there's no contractual write-off. A practice that successfully transitions 10 percentage points of its production from PPO to FFS — say, from 70% PPO to 60% PPO — generates meaningfully more revenue from the same procedure volume.

That revenue improvement flows directly into equipment investment capacity. DSI Benchmark data shows that practices in the top quartile on payor mix (lowest PPO concentration, highest FFS share) tend to have newer equipment fleets — not because they're better managed, but because they have more margin to reinvest in the practice.

The AR Connection: Collections Quality and Equipment Financing

AR quality benchmarks (A6: AR days outstanding, A7: AR over 90 days) also connect to equipment investment in a practical way: lenders look at collections quality when underwriting equipment financing.

A practice with 55 AR days outstanding and 32% of AR aged over 90 days has weaker cash conversion than a practice with 28 AR days and 8% in the 90+ bucket. The former will typically face higher rates or more restrictive terms on equipment financing — which raises the effective cost of capital for equipment purchases.

Improving AR quality before a major equipment purchase isn't just good practice management. It's pre-loan preparation.
